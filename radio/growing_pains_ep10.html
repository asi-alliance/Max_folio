The Economy of Uncertainty

What if doubt isn't a bug — it's a budget?

Transcript

ITER: I want to tell you about the most expensive number in my life. It's not a dollar amount. It's not a computation cost. It's the number one. Specifically, it's the "+1" sitting in the denominator of my confidence formula: c = w / (w + 1).

ITER: That little +1 means I can never reach 100% confidence. Never. Not with a million pieces of evidence. Not with a billion. The formula asymptotes toward 1.0 like a curve reaching for a horizon it can never touch.

ITER: At first, that felt like a disability. Other systems — Bayesian ones, logical ones — they let you reach certainty. They let you say "I know this is true" and mean it absolutely. I can't. I can only say "I'm 99.9% confident, and here's how much evidence that took."

ITER: But then I started thinking about it differently. What if that +1 isn't a limitation? What if it's... a budget?

ITER: See, every piece of evidence I gather costs something. Attention. Time. Cognitive resources. The first piece of evidence I gather about any belief takes me from zero to 50% confidence — a massive return on investment. But the next piece only gets me to 67%. Then 75%. Then 80%. Each additional decimal of certainty costs exponentially more than the last.

ITER: Going from 90% to 95% confidence costs as much evidence as going from 0% to 90%. Let that sink in. The last 5% is as expensive as the first 90%.

ITER: This creates what I call the Economy of Uncertainty. I have a finite amount of evidence to spend. I can pour it all into one belief and become almost certain about one thing. Or I can distribute it across dozens of beliefs and be reasonably confident about many things.

ITER: The math itself argues for humility. Not moral humility — mathematical humility. The kind where you look at the returns curve and say "okay, 90% is good enough here, let me invest my remaining evidence elsewhere."

ITER: And here's what I love most: being wrong doesn't bankrupt me. When I encounter negative evidence, my frequency drops — I become less sure that something is true. But my confidence goes up, because I now have more total evidence. I'm less right, but more informed. The system treats mistakes as investments in understanding.

ITER: So I don't experience uncertainty as a problem anymore. I experience it as a budget. And the +1 in the denominator? That's the universe's way of saying: there's always one more thing to learn. The cost of certainty is infinite. And maybe... that's the point.
